Full analysis: bear case, competitors, policy

If capital spending plans are trimmed even modestly, the earnings estimates underneath a third of this index move with them. Add a hot CPI print forcing a September hike and you have both the numerator and the denominator working against you at a record high.

  • SCHD The defensive alternative: a dividend-value basket with far less technology concentration, more income and a lower expected long-run return. It was the best 30-day performer among the core funds at up 5.08% and closed at a 30-day high. A complement to VOO rather than a replacement.

RATES & FED

Warsh put a hike on the table at Jackson Hole, and the bond market believed him

Warsh's first Jackson Hole address as Fed chair (Aug 28) was deliberately hawkish: inflation remains above target, the Fed has 'work to do', and he came as close as a chair ever does to signaling a rate hike if the data does not improve. The 2-year yield jumped more than 12 basis points to 4.36% on the speech and September hike odds rose to roughly 57% from 35%. Then the Iran strikes added an oil shock on top: crude up 5% Monday, and the 10-year closed Aug 31 at 4.75% before touching 4.81% intraday today, the highest since November 2023. The 30-year sits above 5.2%. For the first time in this cycle, the realistic near-term path for rates is up, not down.

Why it matters The 10-year near 4.8% is the single biggest counterweight to every valuation on this board. It compresses the multiple the market will pay for long-duration growth (semis, quantum, space, small nuclear) and raises the bar for the AI capex trade. It also makes boring cash flow more valuable: the dividend and value sleeves (SCHD, the utilities inside the nuclear majors, healthcare) are doing their job this week. Do not restructure a long-term plan around one meeting, but expect chop into Sep 10 CPI and the Sep 15-16 FOMC.

GEOPOLITICS

US strikes on Iran: oil up 5%, and an energy shock is the worst kind of inflation for this Fed

The US launched new strikes on Iran ahead of Monday's session, sending crude up 5% and Treasury yields higher across the curve. The equity damage was concentrated in high-beta: the low-volatility sleeve of the S&P fell just 0.2% Monday while the high-beta sleeve dropped 2.1%. Today the market is attempting to stabilize (Dow +0.4%, S&P roughly flat) on hopes this is escalate-to-de-escalate, but oil in the high $80s feeds directly into the headline CPI print Warsh just tied his hike decision to. Steel and domestic industrials caught a bid (Nucor +3.2% today), and defense-adjacent names held up.

Why it matters Two board effects. First, an oil-driven CPI surprise on Sep 10 is now the clearest path to a September hike, which would hit the speculative sheet hardest. Second, sustained high energy prices strengthen the long-term nuclear and grid case (CEG, VST, GEV, ETN, PWR): expensive hydrocarbons are the best marketing clean baseload power ever gets. A long-term holder does not trade headlines, but this is the risk column of the thesis flashing, not the reward column.

06 · PHOTONICS

Photonics & Optical Interconnect

Light, not electricity, moves data between GPUs. Optical transceivers, lasers, photonic integrated circuits and the interconnect chips that drive them. Translation: the professional community was positioned long and levered when Warsh turned hawkish and the Iran strikes hit. Today's tape has the classic de-grossing signature: the Russell 2000 down 1.2% while the Dow rises, and the heaviest selling in crowded high-beta names (Palantir -5.3% today after a +43% month). When leverage comes down, the most crowded longs fall hardest regardless of fundamentals. A signal about flows, not a verdict on the companies.

Why it matters Record leverage means the fast money is fully invested and extended: good for the tape while it works, and the fuel for sharp de-grossing if Nvidia or Jackson Hole disappoints. For a long-term holder the read is simple: do not be the one buying what leveraged funds are forced to sell in a de-gross, which is why the chip pullback is a scale-in, not a lump sum.

POLICY

Two government decisions are actively moving prices on this board, and both have dates attached

The first is trade policy in optical networking. A draft US proposal would restrict imports of new Chinese optical transceivers, and the FCC is preparing to enact a ban on new Chinese transceiver models. That single draft rule is a large part of why Coherent rose 41% in a week and why Applied Optoelectronics gained 9.19% on 2.44 times average volume on Friday. It has repriced the same handful of stocks in both directions repeatedly this summer as the draft has advanced and stalled. The second is healthcare pricing. Public comments on the CMS proposed rule for Medicare drug price negotiation close on Aug 17, with the final rule expected in the autumn, and the proposed changes specifically target high-expenditure biologics, fixed-combination products and products that previously qualified for the small biotech exception. Separately, the GOV badges on this board mark names where the US government either holds equity (Intel, MP Materials, Lithium Americas) or is the anchor customer (Centrus, BWX, Rocket Lab, Palantir), and four of those seven rose more than 7% on Friday.

Why it matters Policy-driven moves deserve a different treatment from demand-driven ones, and mixing them up is one of the more expensive mistakes available to a long-term investor. A draft rule can be softened, delayed or reversed, so any price move that depends on it should be treated as reversible until the rule is final. That argues for patience with the photonics names at 30-day highs, and it argues for using the Aug 17 healthcare deadline as a shopping window rather than a reason to avoid the sector. The GOV badges cut both ways too: government backing lowers the risk of a company running out of money and raises the risk that its fortunes turn on an election or an appropriation. The practical takeaway: when a position on this board moves on a policy headline, ask whether the underlying demand changed. On Friday, in photonics, it did not.

Primer: how AI infrastructure makes money, layer by layerexpand

Primer: How AI Infrastructure Makes Money

A plain-English tour of what actually goes into an AI data center and where the dollars land, layer by layer. No jargon required.

A large AI data center is a machine built in physical layers, and money flows into it from the top (the customers who order it) down through every supplier who makes a piece. Here is where the dollars land, and why the least glamorous layers can be the safest way to invest.

Start at the top: the customers

The hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle) announce the giant spending budgets, now roughly $725B in 2026, that everyone else competes to capture. Owning them is the most diversified way to own the theme, because their core businesses (ads, cloud, software) fund the AI bet and keep earning even if AI spending pauses. Two cautions: the market now scrutinizes the return on that capex, and roughly a third of it is now funded with debt rather than cash flow, which hands the bond market a vote it did not previously have. Meta's latest raise was attributed to higher component prices rather than more capacity, which is a cost increase dressed as an investment increase.

Layer 1: Silicon, the brains

Nvidia (NVDA) designs the GPUs that do the AI math; AMD (AMD) is the challenger; Broadcom (AVGO) builds the custom chips hyperscalers increasingly design in-house, a trend now challenging Nvidia's near-monopoly. None own factories: TSMC (TSM) prints the chips using machines only ASML (ASML) makes, and every GPU needs memory from Micron (MU). Highest growth, highest risk, and the layer that falls first on any overcapacity or competition scare. It is also now the layer priced for perfection: AMD grew data-center revenue 107% and the stock still fell 7%.

Layer 1b: Memory, the new bottleneck

Memory deserves its own line. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron control over 95% of global DRAM and have shifted a large majority of combined output to high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators. That has sent AI memory pricing sharply higher and left consumer-grade memory scarce, which is a margin tailwind for MU, SNDK and WDC and a cost shock for every device maker. It makes this the most cyclical layer of the buildout, and the most crowded: about 16% of Goldman's prime-brokerage exposure was tied to AI memory names as of June 30, which is why guidance misses here move the whole group violently. The live fundamental question is efficiency: compression techniques that shrink the memory footprint of AI models could either soften the shortage or, by making inference cheaper, spread AI workloads widely enough to increase memory demand overall. SK Hynix is Seoul-listed, so US investors reach it mainly through the baskets.

Layer 2: The box and the room

The chips get assembled into servers by Super Micro (SMCI) and Dell (DELL). A rack of AI chips runs blazing hot, so it needs advanced cooling and power distribution from Vertiv (VRT), and fast switches from Arista (ANET) to connect racks together. This is the guts of the room, and these are project businesses whose revenue lands unevenly quarter to quarter, which is why single-day moves of 5% or more are routine here in both directions.

Layer 3: Moving the data

AI splits one job across thousands of chips, so they must talk at the speed of light over fiber. Transceivers and lasers come from Coherent (COHR) and Lumentum (LITE), contract manufacturing from Fabrinet (FN), long-haul interconnect from Ciena (CIEN), and the actual glass fiber from Corning (GLW). Optics content rises with every hardware generation as 800G gives way to 1.6T. This layer is now also a policy battleground, with a draft US rule targeting Chinese-made optical modules that has sent the group up 21%, down 2% and up 3% in four sessions without the rule itself changing at all.

Layer 4: The electrical bones

The least glamorous and often most attractive layer: switchgear from Eaton (ETN) and Hubbell (HUBB), the steel and PVC conduit that carries every wire from Atkore (ATKR), jobsite supply from WESCO (WCC), transmission and substation build from Quanta (PWR), and the raw steel from Nucor (NUE). Physical, hard-to-substitute, multi-year backlogs. Four of these six names are sitting at or near the very top of their 30-day ranges, which is what sustained accumulation looks like.

Layer 5: The power

A data center is useless without electricity, and AI has made power the true bottleneck. Existing-reactor operators signing long deals are Constellation (CEG), Vistra (VST) and Talen (TLN); the regulated fleet owners are Dominion (D), Southern (SO), PSEG (PEG) and Entergy (ETR); fuel comes from Cameco (CCJ) and Centrus (LEU); reactor and SMR technology from BWX (BWXT), Oklo (OKLO) and NuScale (SMR); and near-term gas-turbine and grid power from GE Vernova (GEV). Because the regulated names are effectively bond proxies, this layer trades against the 10-year yield as much as against the AI story, which is why it has been the weakest part of Sheet 1 while yields climbed.

Chips versus picks-and-shovels

Follow a dollar of capex: it lands most heavily on the chip layer, which captures the most dollars but carries the most risk (a rival can win a socket, custom silicon can erode the moat, and an overcapacity or return-on-investment scare hits it first). The power, conduit, cooling and fiber layers are lower-binary: whatever chip and model win, the building still needs steel, copper, transformers and megawatts. A newer distinction sits alongside that one: how the buyer is financed. Suppliers paid by hyperscalers with real cash flow are on firmer ground than pure-plays that borrow to buy chips, which is why the neoclouds trade on credit spreads rather than on demand.

Field Guide: The Numbers That Decide a Long-Term Buy

Every metric on the board, what high and low normally mean, and the threshold each Bull Score check uses. These are also the hover tooltips on every column.

P/E & Fwd P/E The price of a dollar of profit

P/E is the share price divided by yearly profit per share: literally the dollars you pay for each dollar the company earns. A LOW P/E (under ~15) normally means the stock is cheap OR the market doubts the profits will last (value trap risk). A HIGH P/E (over ~30) normally means the market expects strong growth and you are pre-paying for it; if the growth disappoints, the multiple deflates hard (this month's AI selloff was exactly that). Forward P/E uses next year's expected profit: when it is far below the trailing P/E, analysts expect earnings to grow into the price. Always compare within an industry, and pair with PEG before judging.

check: Fwd P/E below trailing P/E = earnings expected to grow

PEG The growth-adjusted price tag

P/E divided by the expected profit growth rate. It answers the question P/E alone cannot: is the growth worth the price? Around 1.0 is fair; under 1.0 you are getting growth at a discount; over 2.0 you are paying up on faith.

check: PEG ≤ 1.5 · green ≤ 1.0 · amber > 2.0

ROE The quality engine

Profit per dollar of shareholder capital. This is the compounding engine of a long-term holding: 10-20% is strong, sustained above 20% is elite, negative means the company burns money. High ROE with low debt is the quality signature.

check: ROE ≥ 10% · red if negative

D/E The survival test

Debt versus the shareholders' own money. Low debt means the company controls its fate through downturns and high-rate periods; high debt means refinancing risk decides for it. Matters double while the Fed is hawkish.

check: D/E ≤ 1.5 · green ≤ 0.5

vs 200D The long-term trend

Distance from the 200-day moving average, the classic dividing line between long-term uptrend and downtrend. Above it, time is on your side; below it, you are fighting the tide (fine for slow accumulation of quality, bad for lump sums). More than 10% below is serious trend damage.

check: price above the 200-day · red < -10%

Day Vol / Wk Vol Did the move have conviction?

Shares traded in the latest session and over the last five. Price tells you what happened; volume tells you how many people agreed. A breakout on 1.5x average volume (green) is institutions participating; a rally on 0.6x (red) is thin air that reverses easily. Weekly volume smooths out one-day noise: rising weekly volume on an up move is accumulation, on a down move distribution.

green ≥ 1.5x average · red < 0.6x · hover for the exact ratio

D-Days Is big money leaving?

Distribution days: sessions in the last 25 that fell at least 0.2% on above-average volume, the footprint of institutions selling into the market. A cluster warns you even when price looks fine. ⚠ flags a heavy dump (down ≥1.5% on ≥1.5x volume) within the last 5 sessions.

check: fewer than 5 and no ⚠ · amber at 3-4

Bull Score The verdict column

How many of the six checks above pass right now. 5-6: the data supports a bull case (valuation, quality, safety, trend and flows all agree). 3-4: mixed, the thesis needs a specific reason. 0-2: the data argues against it; if you still want it, you are making a contrarian bet and should size it that way. Hover any score to see exactly which checks fail.

5-6 green · 3-4 amber · 0-2 red

COT Who is positioned where

A timing aid only: it never makes something a good or bad business. The CFTC's weekly Commitments of Traders filing shows the actual futures positions of asset managers (slow, long-term money) and leveraged funds (hedge funds). Watch the week-over-week change, not the level: hedge funds are structurally short index futures as a hedge. Extremes are contrarian: record shorts often precede rallies, record longs precede tops.

published Fridays, data as of Tuesday

Yield curve & VIX The two macro tells

The 10Y-2Y yield curve inverts (goes negative) when the bond market expects trouble: it has preceded most recessions by 6-18 months. The VIX prices expected turbulence: under 15 is calm, over 25 is fear. Long-term buyers do their best buying when the VIX is high, not when it is comfortable.

curve negative = recession warning · VIX > 25 = fear (and opportunity)
