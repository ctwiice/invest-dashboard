AI CREDIT

The AI buildout is now being tested in the credit market, and the neoclouds are where it shows first

Why it matters This is the most important structural development on the page and it deserves more attention than the daily price moves. It is now increasingly funded with borrowed money, which makes it sensitive to interest rates and credit appetite in a way it simply was not before. That has two consequences for a long-term investor. First, it links your AI-infrastructure holdings to the Fed far more tightly than the equity story suggests, which is part of why a September hike matters more this year than last. The neoclouds are the canary here, and today the canary is coughing. Size them accordingly, and treat widening credit spreads as the early-warning indicator to watch rather than the stock prices themselves.

Macro Playbook: Rates, Inflation & Growth

How to position for the current backdrop. Regime read: A rotating market where the selling keeps moving groups, and the AI trade is starting to be judged on credit rather than demand. The quant model reads the market as an uptrend at normal volatility, and the index level supports that: 37 of 70 names are lower and 31 higher, with the broad ETFs moving less than 0.2% while individual names swing 10%. What has changed this week is that the damage keeps relocating. Yesterday chips and photonics were sold and healthcare and industrials bought; today memory and the neoclouds are being sold and photonics, space and chips bought. Three forces define the regime. First, energy relief partly reversed: Brent gained about 2.2% back toward $81 and WTI 1.9% toward $76.60 after Iran published a restrictive draft Hormuz plan and tankers were reportedly attacked in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, taking the 10-year back to about 4.67% and the 30-year to 5.21%. Second, the labour market is quietly cooperative, with claims at 199,000 and July layoff announcements at 33,429, the lowest since July 2024, which gives the Fed room to focus on inflation. Third, and most important structurally, the AI buildout is now being tested in the credit market rather than the demand market: CoreWeave's five-year CDS topped roughly 855 basis points, hyperscaler debt has reached about $700 billion at roughly 32% of capex, and bond coverage ratios have fallen from nearly five times in February to below two by July. Underlying inflation continues to improve (June CPI 3.5% from 4.2%, core 2.6% from 2.8%, unemployment 4.2%, VIX 15.8), so this remains a positioning-and-financing market layered on genuine disinflation, not a growth scare.

The 10-year backed up to about 4.67% today and the 30-year to 5.21% as oil rose, undoing part of yesterday's move toward 4.6%. Do not assume the next move is a cut, because three Fed presidents just voted against that assumption. A flat-to-higher path keeps pressure on anything valued on distant profits, which is why quantum, space, the neoclouds and pre-revenue nuclear stay sized as venture bets. The rate trade is expressed most directly on this board by the regulated utilities in section 08, which are bond proxies pinned at 30-day lows: the group that benefits most if the hike comes off the table, and the one that keeps bleeding if it does not. Improving on the data, wobbling on the input. June CPI 3.5% year over year (down from 4.2%) and core 2.6% (down from 2.8%). Do not press risk into the Aug 12 print in either direction. This week has now delivered two sessions of oil relief and one of oil reversal on nothing but shifting headlines about the same unsigned arrangement, which is a good reason to distrust any single day's read. Own some pricing power and real assets as the hedge: market-priced power operators (CEG, VST, TLN), healthcare that passes costs through (LLY, XLV), and broad exposure via VOO or VTI. Keep some cash available for the print itself rather than deploying everything before it.

Growth and demand

Still strong where it counts, with the caveat that beating is no longer sufficient. SanDisk grew revenue 372% year over year and Western Digital 44% with GAAP net income up more than twelvefold, and both stocks were sold hard on guidance that came in short of aggressive forecasts. AMD's data-center revenue rose 107% yesterday to $6.7 billion and the stock fell 7%. This is the leg of the thesis that is working, so let it work rather than trading around it, but internalise the specific lesson this week has taught three times: demand growth of 100% to 370% was not enough to lift stocks that had already run. The response is not to abandon the theme but to own it through baskets where valuation and guidance risk are spread (SMH is up 1% today while Western Digital falls 11%, and it lost only 1% yesterday while AMD fell 7%), and to size the AI hardware sleeve so a 30% to 45% drawdown is survivable, because that is what its history says it does.

Positioning and financing

The dominant driver, and the financing half is newly important. Hedge funds cut US tech at a record pace into mid-July and have since posted their biggest increase in net technology exposure in more than five years, with positioning near the highest since 2016 and the largest tech overweight versus MSCI World in over five years. Gross leverage is at a record and net leverage near three-year highs, with US long-short net leverage at 55.3%, in the top 11% of the past year. Roughly 16% of Goldman's prime-brokerage exposure was tied to AI memory names as of June 30, which is exactly the group being liquidated today. On the corporate side, hyperscaler debt is near $700 billion at about 32% of capex, CoreWeave's CDS topped roughly 855 basis points, and bond coverage ratios fell from nearly five times in February to below two in July.

Use flow as a timing input only, but treat credit as a genuine early-warning system rather than noise. A rebuilt, highly leveraged book concentrated in the same names means the market is more sensitive to any shock, because leveraged holders sell because they must. Practically: slow purchases in whatever the crowd is most concentrated in (mega-cap AI and now AI memory), keep position sizes where a fast 10% move does not force you to act, and prefer suppliers paid by cash-rich buyers over pure-plays that borrow to buy chips. The Aug 7 COT release is the next checkpoint, specifically whether the record leveraged short in the 10-year got any smaller.

Policy

Still a first-order driver, and increasingly a source of noise rather than information. The draft FCC-led ban on Chinese optical transceiver modules is in rule drafting, and the optics names have now moved up 21%, down 2.18% and up 3.06% across four sessions without a single change to the rule. Medicare drug price negotiation is in its third cycle, covering 15 drugs worth about $27 billion or 6% of Part B and Part D spending, reaching Part B for the first time, with comments closing Aug 17 and prices effective January 2028. Government equity stakes and anchor-customer relationships continue to underwrite Intel, MP Materials, Lithium Americas, Centrus, BWX Technologies, Rocket Lab and Palantir.

Let policy adjust your sizing, never form your thesis. Three opposite moves in the same group in four sessions, with no change in the underlying rule, is as clean a demonstration as exists that these are probability repricings rather than value changes. Prefer businesses that would work without the rule and get helped by it (Coherent, Fabrinet, Corning) over ones that only work because of it. Note that Corning is the one optics name that has not participated, sitting 13% below its 50-day, which is where policy-driven enthusiasm has left something behind. Keep the GOV-badged names a slice rather than a concentration, since the same administration that created the floor can remove it.

Why it matters The optics round trip is the cleanest evidence available that policy-driven moves are repricings of probability rather than of business value, and it has now repeated three times in four sessions. Nothing has been enacted. The discipline is to let policy adjust your sizing but never form your thesis: prefer businesses that would work without the rule and get helped by it (Coherent, Fabrinet, Corning) over ones whose case depends on it, and treat a policy jump as a prompt to review a position rather than to buy one at a 20% premium. Primer: how AI infrastructure makes money, layer by layerexpand

Primer: How AI Infrastructure Makes Money

A plain-English tour of what actually goes into an AI data center and where the dollars land, layer by layer. No jargon required.

A large AI data center is a machine built in physical layers, and money flows into it from the top (the customers who order it) down through every supplier who makes a piece. Here is where the dollars land, and why the least glamorous layers can be the safest way to invest.

Start at the top: the customers

The hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle) announce the giant spending budgets, now roughly $725B in 2026, that everyone else competes to capture. Owning them is the most diversified way to own the theme, because their core businesses (ads, cloud, software) fund the AI bet and keep earning even if AI spending pauses. Two cautions: the market now scrutinizes the return on that capex, and roughly a third of it is now funded with debt rather than cash flow, which hands the bond market a vote it did not previously have. Meta's latest raise was attributed to higher component prices rather than more capacity, which is a cost increase dressed as an investment increase.

Layer 1: Silicon, the brains

Nvidia (NVDA) designs the GPUs that do the AI math; AMD (AMD) is the challenger; Broadcom (AVGO) builds the custom chips hyperscalers increasingly design in-house, a trend now challenging Nvidia's near-monopoly. None own factories: TSMC (TSM) prints the chips using machines only ASML (ASML) makes, and every GPU needs memory from Micron (MU). Highest growth, highest risk, and the layer that falls first on any overcapacity or competition scare. It is also now the layer priced for perfection: AMD grew data-center revenue 107% and the stock still fell 7%.

Layer 1b: Memory, the new bottleneck

Memory deserves its own line. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron control over 95% of global DRAM and have shifted a large majority of combined output to high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators. That has sent AI memory pricing sharply higher and left consumer-grade memory scarce, which is a margin tailwind for MU, SNDK and WDC and a cost shock for every device maker. It makes this the most cyclical layer of the buildout, and the most crowded: about 16% of Goldman's prime-brokerage exposure was tied to AI memory names as of June 30, which is why guidance misses here move the whole group violently. The live fundamental question is efficiency: compression techniques that shrink the memory footprint of AI models could either soften the shortage or, by making inference cheaper, spread AI workloads widely enough to increase memory demand overall. SK Hynix is Seoul-listed, so US investors reach it mainly through the baskets.

Layer 2: The box and the room

The chips get assembled into servers by Super Micro (SMCI) and Dell (DELL). A rack of AI chips runs blazing hot, so it needs advanced cooling and power distribution from Vertiv (VRT), and fast switches from Arista (ANET) to connect racks together. This is the guts of the room, and these are project businesses whose revenue lands unevenly quarter to quarter, which is why single-day moves of 5% or more are routine here in both directions.

Layer 3: Moving the data

AI splits one job across thousands of chips, so they must talk at the speed of light over fiber. Transceivers and lasers come from Coherent (COHR) and Lumentum (LITE), contract manufacturing from Fabrinet (FN), long-haul interconnect from Ciena (CIEN), and the actual glass fiber from Corning (GLW). Optics content rises with every hardware generation as 800G gives way to 1.6T. This layer is now also a policy battleground, with a draft US rule targeting Chinese-made optical modules that has sent the group up 21%, down 2% and up 3% in four sessions without the rule itself changing at all.

Layer 4: The electrical bones

The least glamorous and often most attractive layer: switchgear from Eaton (ETN) and Hubbell (HUBB), the steel and PVC conduit that carries every wire from Atkore (ATKR), jobsite supply from WESCO (WCC), transmission and substation build from Quanta (PWR), and the raw steel from Nucor (NUE). Physical, hard-to-substitute, multi-year backlogs. Four of these six names are sitting at or near the very top of their 30-day ranges, which is what sustained accumulation looks like.

Layer 5: The power

A data center is useless without electricity, and AI has made power the true bottleneck. Existing-reactor operators signing long deals are Constellation (CEG), Vistra (VST) and Talen (TLN); the regulated fleet owners are Dominion (D), Southern (SO), PSEG (PEG) and Entergy (ETR); fuel comes from Cameco (CCJ) and Centrus (LEU); reactor and SMR technology from BWX (BWXT), Oklo (OKLO) and NuScale (SMR); and near-term gas-turbine and grid power from GE Vernova (GEV). Because the regulated names are effectively bond proxies, this layer trades against the 10-year yield as much as against the AI story, which is why it has been the weakest part of Sheet 1 while yields climbed.

Chips versus picks-and-shovels

Follow a dollar of capex: it lands most heavily on the chip layer, which captures the most dollars but carries the most risk (a rival can win a socket, custom silicon can erode the moat, and an overcapacity or return-on-investment scare hits it first). The power, conduit, cooling and fiber layers are lower-binary: whatever chip and model win, the building still needs steel, copper, transformers and megawatts. A newer distinction sits alongside that one: how the buyer is financed. Suppliers paid by hyperscalers with real cash flow are on firmer ground than pure-plays that borrow to buy chips, which is why the neoclouds trade on credit spreads rather than on demand.

Field Guide: The Numbers That Decide a Long-Term Buy

Every metric on the board, what high and low normally mean, and the threshold each Bull Score check uses. These are also the hover tooltips on every column.

P/E & Fwd P/E The price of a dollar of profit

P/E is the share price divided by yearly profit per share: literally the dollars you pay for each dollar the company earns. A LOW P/E (under ~15) normally means the stock is cheap OR the market doubts the profits will last (value trap risk). A HIGH P/E (over ~30) normally means the market expects strong growth and you are pre-paying for it; if the growth disappoints, the multiple deflates hard (this month's AI selloff was exactly that). Forward P/E uses next year's expected profit: when it is far below the trailing P/E, analysts expect earnings to grow into the price. Always compare within an industry, and pair with PEG before judging.

check: Fwd P/E below trailing P/E = earnings expected to grow

PEG The growth-adjusted price tag

P/E divided by the expected profit growth rate. It answers the question P/E alone cannot: is the growth worth the price? Around 1.0 is fair; under 1.0 you are getting growth at a discount; over 2.0 you are paying up on faith.

check: PEG ≤ 1.5 · green ≤ 1.0 · amber > 2.0

ROE The quality engine

Profit per dollar of shareholder capital. This is the compounding engine of a long-term holding: 10-20% is strong, sustained above 20% is elite, negative means the company burns money. High ROE with low debt is the quality signature.

check: ROE ≥ 10% · red if negative

D/E The survival test

Debt versus the shareholders' own money. Low debt means the company controls its fate through downturns and high-rate periods; high debt means refinancing risk decides for it. Matters double while the Fed is hawkish.

check: D/E ≤ 1.5 · green ≤ 0.5

vs 200D The long-term trend

Distance from the 200-day moving average, the classic dividing line between long-term uptrend and downtrend. Above it, time is on your side; below it, you are fighting the tide (fine for slow accumulation of quality, bad for lump sums). More than 10% below is serious trend damage.

check: price above the 200-day · red < -10%

D-Days Is big money leaving?

Distribution days: sessions in the last 25 that fell at least 0.2% on above-average volume, the footprint of institutions selling into the market. A cluster warns you even when price looks fine. ⚠ flags a heavy dump (down ≥1.5% on ≥1.5x volume) within the last 5 sessions.

check: fewer than 5 and no ⚠ · amber at 3-4

Bull Score The verdict column

How many of the six checks above pass right now. 5-6: the data supports a bull case (valuation, quality, safety, trend and flows all agree). 3-4: mixed, the thesis needs a specific reason. 0-2: the data argues against it; if you still want it, you are making a contrarian bet and should size it that way.
