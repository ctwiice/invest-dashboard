Third, CMS moved the third round of Medicare drug price negotiation forward, reaching Part B drugs for the first time, with comments closing Aug 17. Alongside these, the standing government positions on the board have not changed: Washington holds equity in Intel, MP Materials and Lithium Americas, and is the anchor customer for Centrus, BWX Technologies, Rocket Lab and Palantir. Those GOV-badged names were broadly strong today, with Intel up 9.58%, MP Materials up 7.73% and Palantir up 26.59%.

Why it matters Policy as a moat cuts both ways, and today was the pleasant direction. An import ban on a foreign competitor hands domestic suppliers pricing power they did not earn commercially, which is genuinely valuable while it lasts and can be reversed by the same pen that wrote it. The practical discipline is to treat a policy-driven jump as a reason to review a position, never as the thesis itself. A business that only works because of a draft regulation is a bet on politics; one that would work anyway and gets helped by the regulation is an investment.

Primer: how AI infrastructure makes money, layer by layerexpand

Primer: How AI Infrastructure Makes Money

A plain-English tour of what actually goes into an AI data center and where the dollars land, layer by layer. No jargon required.

A large AI data center is a machine built in physical layers, and money flows into it from the top (the customers who order it) down through every supplier who makes a piece. Here is where the dollars land, and why the least glamorous layers can be the safest way to invest.

Start at the top: the customers

The hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle) announce the giant spending budgets, now roughly $725B in 2026, that everyone else competes to capture. Owning them is the most diversified way to own the theme, because their core businesses (ads, cloud, software) fund the AI bet and keep earning even if AI spending pauses. Two cautions surfaced this quarter: the market now scrutinizes the return on that capex (Alphabet raised its 2026 spend and its free cash flow turned negative, and Meta's net income fell 14% while expenses rose 55%), and the group is increasingly tapping external financing, which makes the whole chain more rate-sensitive.

Layer 1: Silicon, the brains

Nvidia (NVDA) designs the GPUs that do the AI math; AMD (AMD) is the challenger; Broadcom (AVGO) builds the custom chips hyperscalers increasingly design in-house, a trend now challenging Nvidia's near-monopoly. None own factories: TSMC (TSM) prints the chips using machines only ASML (ASML) makes, and every GPU needs memory from Micron (MU). Highest growth, highest risk, and the layer that falls first on any overcapacity or competition scare.

Layer 1b: Memory, the new bottleneck

Memory deserves its own line now. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron control over 95% of global DRAM and have shifted roughly 93% of combined output to high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators. That has sent AI memory pricing sharply higher and left consumer-grade memory scarce, which is why smartphone volumes are forecast to fall about 13% this year and PCs about 11%. It is a margin tailwind for MU, SNDK and WDC and a cost shock for every device maker, and it makes this the most cyclical layer of the whole buildout. SK Hynix is Seoul-listed, so US investors reach it mainly through the baskets.

Layer 2: The box and the room

The chips get assembled into servers by Super Micro (SMCI) and Dell (DELL). A rack of AI chips runs blazing hot, so it needs advanced cooling and power distribution from Vertiv (VRT), and fast switches from Arista (ANET) to connect racks together. This is the guts of the room, and Vertiv's 17% single-day drop after a raised guide is a reminder that these are project businesses whose revenue lands unevenly quarter to quarter.

Layer 3: Moving the data

AI splits one job across thousands of chips, so they must talk at the speed of light over fiber. Transceivers and lasers come from Coherent (COHR) and Lumentum (LITE), long-haul interconnect from Ciena (CIEN), and the actual glass fiber from Corning (GLW). Optics content rises with every hardware generation, though these names trade with the chip complex on risk-off days and have been among the hardest hit in this drawdown.

Layer 4: The electrical bones

The least glamorous and often most attractive layer: switchgear from Eaton (ETN) and Hubbell (HUBB), the steel and PVC conduit that carries every wire from Atkore (ATKR), jobsite supply from WESCO (WCC), transmission and substation build from Quanta (PWR), and the raw steel from Nucor (NUE). Physical, hard-to-substitute, multi-year backlogs. This layer had held up through most of the AI selloff, so heavy institutional selling arriving here is worth watching as a signal about the pace of the buildout itself.

Layer 5: The power

A data center is useless without electricity, and AI has made power the true bottleneck. Existing-reactor operators signing long deals are Constellation (CEG) and Vistra (VST); fuel comes from Cameco (CCJ); reactor and SMR technology from BWX (BWXT), Oklo (OKLO) and NuScale (SMR); and near-term gas-turbine and grid power from GE Vernova (GEV). The operators have generally held up better than the developers, because they sell electricity today while the developers are selling a milestone in 2028.

Chips versus picks-and-shovels

Follow a dollar of capex: it lands most heavily on the chip layer, which captures the most dollars but carries the most risk (a rival can win a socket, custom silicon can erode the moat, and an overcapacity or return-on-investment scare hits it first). The power, conduit, cooling and fiber layers are lower-binary: whatever chip and model win, the building still needs steel, copper, transformers and megawatts. That is the trade-off, highest upside in silicon, more durable margins of safety in the infrastructure beneath it, though this week showed that in a broad de-grossing everything sells together for a while.

Field Guide: The Numbers That Decide a Long-Term Buy

Every metric on the board, what high and low normally mean, and the threshold each Bull Score check uses. These are also the hover tooltips on every column.

P/E & Fwd P/E The price of a dollar of profit

P/E is the share price divided by yearly profit per share: literally the dollars you pay for each dollar the company earns. A LOW P/E (under ~15) normally means the stock is cheap OR the market doubts the profits will last (value trap risk). A HIGH P/E (over ~30) normally means the market expects strong growth and you are pre-paying for it; if the growth disappoints, the multiple deflates hard (this month's AI selloff was exactly that). Forward P/E uses next year's expected profit: when it is far below the trailing P/E, analysts expect earnings to grow into the price. Always compare within an industry, and pair with PEG before judging.

check: Fwd P/E below trailing P/E = earnings expected to grow

PEG The growth-adjusted price tag

P/E divided by the expected profit growth rate. It answers the question P/E alone cannot: is the growth worth the price? Around 1.0 is fair; under 1.0 you are getting growth at a discount; over 2.0 you are paying up on faith.

check: PEG ≤ 1.5 · green ≤ 1.0 · amber > 2.0

ROE The quality engine

Profit per dollar of shareholder capital. This is the compounding engine of a long-term holding: 10-20% is strong, sustained above 20% is elite, negative means the company burns money. High ROE with low debt is the quality signature.

check: ROE ≥ 10% · red if negative

D/E The survival test

Debt versus the shareholders' own money. Low debt means the company controls its fate through downturns and high-rate periods; high debt means refinancing risk decides for it. Matters double while the Fed is hawkish.

check: D/E ≤ 1.5 · green ≤ 0.5

vs 200D The long-term trend

Distance from the 200-day moving average, the classic dividing line between long-term uptrend and downtrend. Above it, time is on your side; below it, you are fighting the tide (fine for slow accumulation of quality, bad for lump sums). More than 10% below is serious trend damage.

check: price above the 200-day · red < -10%

D-Days Is big money leaving?

Distribution days: sessions in the last 25 that fell at least 0.2% on above-average volume, the footprint of institutions selling into the market. A cluster warns you even when price looks fine. ⚠ flags a heavy dump (down ≥1.5% on ≥1.5x volume) within the last 5 sessions.

check: fewer than 5 and no ⚠ · amber at 3-4

Bull Score The verdict column

How many of the six checks above pass right now. 5-6: the data supports a bull case (valuation, quality, safety, trend and flows all agree). 3-4: mixed, the thesis needs a specific reason. 0-2: the data argues against it; if you still want it, you are making a contrarian bet and should size it that way. COT Who is positioned where

A timing aid only: it never makes something a good or bad business. The CFTC's weekly Commitments of Traders filing shows the actual futures positions of asset managers (slow, long-term money) and leveraged funds (hedge funds). Watch the week-over-week change, not the level: hedge funds are structurally short index futures as a hedge. Extremes are contrarian: record shorts often precede rallies, record longs precede tops.

Yield curve & VIX The two macro tells

The 10Y-2Y yield curve inverts (goes negative) when the bond market expects trouble: it has preceded most recessions by 6-18 months. The VIX prices expected turbulence: under 15 is calm, over 25 is fear. Long-term buyers do their best buying when the VIX is high, not when it is comfortable.
